\section{Limitations}

The external cohort is modest relative to the number of sources of EEG
heterogeneity. HBN and MIPDB differ in recruitment, acquisition hardware,
recording procedures, demographic composition, and potentially data quality.
External performance therefore combines representation accessibility with
domain shift; it cannot isolate a single causal source of error.

The inference covers ten optimization seeds and four prespecified heads. It
does not cover all initializations, regularization strengths, checkpoint rules,
architectures, REVE layers, or pooling functions. The bootstrap quantifies
uncertainty under the implemented seed-and-subject resampling scheme, while the
sign-flip permutation test relies on exchangeable or symmetric seed-level signs
under its null model. With only ten seeds, the permutation distribution is
necessarily discrete and should not be read as population-level evidence over
all future training procedures.

Common optimization settings provide a matched training budget, not the best
achievable configuration for each head. Architecture-specific tuning could
change the comparison and would require a development-only selection rule and
a separate evaluation protocol. MAE, RMSE, and calibration rankings are
descriptive; no metric-agnostic or clinical superiority claim follows from
them.

The capacity--data extension is secondary and exploratory. It samples only
three nested HBN training sizes, two richer candidate heads, and the same ten
seeds; it does not evaluate the full available HBN cohort or establish a
scaling law. Its training-size contrasts also inherit the finite MIPDB cohort
and the same cross-cohort shift as the primary analysis. Reusing MIPDB after
the primary analysis does not restore an untouched holdout. The additional
prediction rows and optimization seeds do not increase the number of
independent external subjects, and the two extensions are not independent
replications of the primary finding.

The layer-wise analysis is also secondary and exploratory. It compares only
three earlier layers with the final layer, uses the same ten seeds and
\LayerwiseSubjectCount{} external subjects, and trains a separate linear head at
each depth. Layer depth
is therefore not a causal intervention on the encoder, and the analysis cannot
establish a general ordering of representation quality across layers.

The ds006780 transfer has a separate cohort but remains a secondary extension
motivated by the earlier results. Its public recordings use a different
BioSemi-64 acquisition context, leave the EEG reference undocumented, and lack
subject-specific electrode coordinates; the analysis therefore uses a fixed
canonical montage approximation and preserves acquisition reference rather
than claiming harmonized hardware. It evaluates only two heads and two nested
training sizes. Although all ten $n=800$ seed contrasts were positive, the
subject-aware interval width exceeded the prespecified precision threshold, so
the extension cannot establish stable superiority or a general cross-cohort
effect.

The cohort flow is reported in aggregate, but the compact repository bundle
does not retain participant-level demographics, recording counts, or window
counts. No additional demographic or recording-level supplement is included;
the available aggregate cohort flow does not characterize every potential
source of recruitment or acquisition imbalance.

The MIPDB primary analysis is restricted to ages within HBN training support.
The extrapolation cohort was not included in the confirmatory comparison, so no
claim is made about out-of-support ages. The published REVE source list does not
name MIPDB among pretraining datasets, but absence from that list is evidence,
not a cryptographic guarantee of encoder-level independence. The same published
inventory includes HBN releases, so HBN development results are conditional on
an encoder that may have encountered HBN recordings during self-supervised
pretraining; they should not be described as an encoder-unseen test.

Chronological-age decoding is not clinical brain-age validation. Negative
R-squared and non-ideal calibration in this external
cohort show that correlation can coexist with substantial absolute error and
systematic scaling. Finally, the present result does not establish equivalence,
a true zero effect, or the absence of benefit from an untested method.
